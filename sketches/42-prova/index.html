What happens when pollution is fought with another change to the air?

In January 2025, Bangkok's air filled with fine dust (PM2.5) from burning, traffic, industry and smoke blown in from the region. Calm, cool weather kept it close to the ground. To clear it, the authorities flew planes over the city and sprayed cold water and dry ice into the sky. This page shows what was sprayed, what it was meant to do, and what happened to the dust.

What was sprayed

Cold water, ice and dry ice (frozen carbon dioxide, about −70 °C), about a tonne per load, from small aircraft of the Department of Royal Rainmaking. From 24 January they flew over inner Bangkok twice a day, at 10:00 and 14:00, below 3,000 ft (about 900 m); AFP reported releases from about 1,500 m. From 26 January the Air Force added two larger planes carrying about 2,000 kg of dry ice or other material each. Planes had already been spraying outside the city earlier in January.

What was the goal

On these mornings a layer of warmer air sat above the city like a lid, trapping the dust underneath. The authorities said the cold spray would open "channels" in that lid so the dust could rise and spread out. In the view, this is the opening that appears in the purple while a plane sprays. It shows the claim, not a measurement.

What happened to PM2.5

Measured each morning across Bangkok, in µg/m³ (the Thai daily limit is 37.5):

22 Jan 62.6 · 23 Jan 71.0 · 24 Jan 88.4 (flights over the city start) · 25 Jan 73.4 · 26 Jan 58.6 · 27 Jan 41.0 · 28 Jan 26.0

So the dust fell by more than two thirds in four days after the flights began. But the weather changed at the same time: a cold air mass arrived on 26 January and the wind picked up on 27 January. The department said its flights broke the lid; the pollution control agency and the governor pointed to the wind. No one published measurements taken just before and just after a flight, so no one can say how much of the drop came from the spraying.

How to read the view

Purple dots: dust in the air (measured)
More dots means more PM2.5. The amount follows the morning measurements; in between, it is smoothed. The tall purple bars are the worst districts each morning. The dots are symbols, not real particles.
Red: the spray (reported)
A plane arrives, climbs and sprays in a spiral at the reported times. The exact paths were never published, so the route is drawn, not traced.
The opening in the purple: the claimed effect
What the authorities said the spray would do. It is not measured. The overall amount of purple still follows the measurements only.
Grey dashed line: how deep the air is being mixed
From a weather reconstruction (ERA5). Low in the mornings, when the dust is trapped; high in the afternoons.

    Sources and limits

    • PM2.5, observed. BMA morning bulletins (05:00–07:00, reported about 07:00), via The Nation and BMA pages. Not 24-hour averages. 26 January (58.6) comes from the official bulletin cited in the research brief and is not independently checked here. District bars show only the highest districts each report names, so the spatial field elsewhere is not known; the purple cloud is the same across the city.
    • Weather, reanalysed and modelled. ERA5 via Open-Meteo (2 m temperature, 10 m wind, boundary-layer height) at 13.75° N 100.50° E, one grid point for the whole city. Pressure-level temperatures from Open-Meteo's archived model runs are kept in the data but not drawn. Licence CC BY 4.0. Fetched 16 November 2026; processed with tools/prepare-data.mjs.
    • Flights, reported. Altitudes, times, materials and aircraft as reported (see the archive). The sources disagree on altitude and on when Bangkok flights were first permitted. No daily flight log for Bangkok exists in the sources found.
    • City, cartographic and schematic. River and city outline from Natural Earth (public domain); districts from geoBoundaries (Royal Thai Survey Department, OCHA ROAP; CC BY 3.0 IGO). Mahanakhon and Baiyoke Tower II are placed and sized from CTBUH and Wikipedia. All other buildings are generated and are not real buildings.
