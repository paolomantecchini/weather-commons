What happens when environmental degradation is addressed through further environmental intervention?

In January 2025, fine particulate matter (PM2.5) built up over Bangkok. Burning, traffic, industry and regional transport changed the air; weak ventilation and temperature inversions held it near the ground. Authorities then flew aircraft releasing cold water and dry ice into the atmosphere, hoping to break the inversion and let the dust escape. This sketch sets the two modifications side by side and asks whether a modification can restore an earlier condition, or whether it redistributes consequences and makes further intervention normal. It does not assume an answer.

Reading the view

Purple · existing PM2.5 (observed)
Symbolic. The number of particles follows the BMA morning bulletin: visualDensity = clamp(C / 100 µg/m³, 0, 1), so 88.4 µg/m³ shows 88% of the particles and 26 µg/m³ shows 26%. Particle size, number and speed are not physical. Between the 07:00 bulletins the value is interpolated for display only (switch it off to see steps). Particles are drawn mostly below the ERA5 mixing height and drift with ERA5 surface wind; both are visual choices, not a dispersion model.
Amber · temperature and inversion layers (reanalysed and modelled)
Bands appear wherever a model level is warmer than the one below it (an inversion), with opacity following the temperature difference. The dashed amber plane is ERA5 boundary-layer height, the depth of air the model expects to be mixed.
Blue/cyan · substances released by aircraft (reported, schematic)
Shown only in the reported 10:00 and 14:00 slots from 24 January. The path, the plume and the aircraft are schematic: no flight track, release time or quantity for a specific Bangkok flight has been published. The altitude band spans the reported figures (below 3,000 ft; about 1,500 m). The cyan plume does not touch the purple particles. The purple follows the measurements alone, so the view never shows the flights clearing the air.

Event

Morning PM2.5 rose from 54.1 µg/m³ on 20 January to 88.4 on 24 January, more than twice the Thai 24-hour standard of 37.5, then fell to 26 on 28 January. On 22 January 53 schools closed; on 24 January, 352. Free public transport ran from 25 to 31 January. The surrounding reports name burning as a main source; the department running the flights said stopping burning was the most effective solution.

Simulate

Nothing here is a physical simulation. The view is a mapping of three separate records onto one shared clock: the morning PM2.5 bulletins (observed), hourly ERA5 reanalysis and archived model temperatures at six heights (reanalysed and modelled), and the published accounts of the flights (reported). Their relationship in the view is visual, not causal. Heights are exaggerated eight times; labels give true metres.

Situate

The Department of Royal Rainmaking and Agricultural Aviation ran PM2.5 operations from December 2024 with aircraft at regional bases. An AFP photograph dated 15 January shows icy water sprayed over Bangkok's outskirts; on 6 January Khaosod reported permission for flights over the city. On 24 January the government announced an expanded phase with flights in inner Bangkok airspace. The Royal Thai Air Force joined from 26 January. Before and during this, weather agencies tracked a cold air mass and stronger winds arriving on 26–27 January.

Narrate

The air did clear. The view shows what can be said about why. The flights began as the morning values peaked; the values fell over the following four days; the reanalysis shows the morning inversion disappearing and the mixing height rising to 580 m on 27 January, as surface wind reached 5–6 m/s from the east-northeast that morning. The department claimed it broke the inversion. Weather agencies and the governor credited wind. No public before-and-after measurement isolates the effect of any flight. A viewer can see the coincidence; they should not conclude the cause.
